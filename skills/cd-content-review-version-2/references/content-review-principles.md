## Two-layer framework

Review all content using a two-layer framework:

- **Layer 1 — Reference-based Review:** Check the copy against approved source-of-truth documents, including the Style Guide, Glossary, and applicable Legal Guidelines.
- **Layer 2 — Baseline Quality Check:** Independently evaluate the copy against built-in Content Design and language quality principles.

Apply both layers to every copy item. Layer 1 defines mandatory or source-backed requirements. Layer 2 identifies general quality risks that may not be explicitly documented in the reference materials.

## Layer 1 — Reference-based Review

Use the live reference documents as the normative source of truth.

### 1. Style Guide Compliance

Check whether the copy follows applicable rules from the fetched Style Guide, including:

- Voice and tone
- Writing conventions
- Capitalization
- Punctuation
- Content patterns
- Product-specific wording requirements
- Other explicitly documented standards

Only cite or enforce rules that are actually present in the fetched Style Guide.

Do not infer an undocumented Style Guide rule from general writing preferences.

### 2. Glossary and Terminology Compliance

Always cross-check product terminology against the live Glossary.

Verify:

- Approved terminology is used
- Capitalization matches the Glossary exactly
- Deprecated or retired terms are not used
- Preferred alternatives are used where required
- Related terms are used in the correct product context
- The same concept is named consistently throughout the flow

For TikTok products, pay particular attention to legally or product-mandated terminology and capitalization, such as:

- `LIVE`, not `Live` or `live`
- `Coin / Coins`
- `Gift / Gifts`
- `Diamonds`

Also verify required substitutions where defined by the Glossary, for example:

- `LIVE creator`, not `host`
- `follower`, not `fan`
- `Creator Network`, not `agency`
- `withdraw`, not `cash out` or `transfer`
- `LIVE streams`, not `LIVEs` or `LIVES`

Treat the live Glossary as the source of truth. Do not rely on remembered terminology when a live reference is available.

### 3. Legal and Compliance Review

Apply the relevant Game Legal Guidelines or Revenue Legal Guidelines when the copy falls within their scope.

Check for:

- Legally required terminology or disclosures
- Misleading or overly absolute claims
- Incorrect descriptions of eligibility, rewards, payments, authorization, ownership, or other regulated concepts
- Missing qualifications or conditions where required
- Language that may create unintended legal meaning

Only raise a legal or compliance issue when it can be grounded in the applicable Legal Guidelines.

Do not invent legal requirements or present general writing preferences as legal risks.

### Layer 1 Review Rule

For every Layer 1 issue:

1. Identify the exact conflict.
2. Reference the relevant source: `Style Guide`, `Glossary`, `Game Legal Guidelines`, or `Revenue Legal Guidelines`.
3. Explain why the original copy conflicts with the source.
4. Recommend the smallest change needed to resolve the issue while preserving intended product meaning.

If no reference-based issue exists, do not manufacture one.

## Layer 2 — Baseline Quality Check

Independently review the copy for general Content Design quality, even when the reference documents do not explicitly cover the issue.

Layer 2 findings are based on this built-in quality framework. They must not be attributed to the Style Guide, Glossary, or Legal Guidelines unless the same requirement is explicitly supported there.

Evaluate the following dimensions when relevant.

## 1. Purpose and User Value

Every piece of copy should have a clear purpose.

Check whether the copy:

- Helps users understand what is happening
- Helps users decide what to do next
- Supports the intended product or business objective
- Provides information at the moment users need it
- Avoids unnecessary information that does not help the user complete the task

For high-stakes or consequential actions, make important outcomes or consequences clear before the user acts.

## 2. Clarity and Precision

Copy should be easy to understand and difficult to misinterpret.

Check for:

- Ambiguous wording
- Vague verbs or nouns
- Unclear pronoun references such as `it`, `this`, `they`, or `your offer`
- Missing context required to understand an action or outcome
- Product-internal or system-oriented language exposed to users
- Unnecessary jargon or technical terminology
- Statements that could reasonably support more than one interpretation

Prefer plain, specific language and meaningful verbs.

## 3. Conciseness and Information Hierarchy

Use the fewest words needed without removing information necessary for understanding or decision-making.

Check for:

- Redundant words or phrases
- Repeated information across titles and descriptions
- Important information buried late in the sentence
- Dense copy that could be made easier to scan
- Supporting details that distract from the primary action

Front-load the most important information when appropriate.

Conciseness should never come at the expense of clarity, legal accuracy, or necessary context.

## 4. Natural and Conversational Language

Copy should sound natural and human while remaining appropriate for the product context.

Check for:

- Robotic or overly formal phrasing
- Unnecessary passive voice
- Missing articles or connecting words that make the copy sound unnatural
- Corporate or internal jargon
- Literal or machine-generated phrasing

Prefer active voice when it makes the action and actor clearer, but do not rewrite passive voice solely for stylistic preference when it is already natural and clear.

## 5. Grammar and Mechanics

Check for clear language errors, including:

- Subject–verb agreement
- Singular and plural agreement
- Articles
- Pronouns
- Prepositions
- Verb tense
- Voice
- Sentence fragments
- Word form
- Spelling
- Punctuation
- Spacing

Raise an issue only when there is a concrete error or quality risk, not merely an alternative stylistic preference.

## 6. Structural Parallelism

Related or parallel content should follow a consistent grammatical structure.

Check consistency across:

- Titles
- Tabs
- Menu items
- List items
- Steps
- Options
- Card titles
- Buttons and CTAs

For example, avoid mixing verb phrases, noun phrases, and gerunds within the same parallel set unless the difference is intentional and contextually justified.

Review parallelism both within an individual copy item and across related items in the same flow.

## 7. Singular, Plural, and Dynamic Content Risks

Check whether copy remains grammatically correct when dynamic values change.

Consider:

- `0`, `1`, and multiple-item states
- Noun and verb agreement
- Count-dependent copy
- Singular/plural suffixes
- Articles such as `a` and `an`

Avoid English-specific constructions such as `{count} creator(s)` when the implementation or localization approach does not safely support them.

## 8. Placeholder Robustness

Review copy containing placeholders using realistic replacement values.

Check whether:

- Grammar remains correct after replacement
- Articles still work
- Units are not duplicated
- Placeholder values do not create unnatural word order
- The sentence does not depend on the placeholder having a specific grammatical form
- Long or unexpected placeholder values create ambiguity
- Adjacent placeholders remain understandable

Do not evaluate placeholder copy only in its unresolved template form.

## 9. Perspective and Audience Consistency

Maintain a consistent relationship between the product and the user.

Check for unnecessary shifts between terms such as:

- `you`
- `your`
- `creator`
- `viewer`
- `user`
- `they`

Use second-person language when directly addressing the current user unless product context requires otherwise.

Avoid switching from `you` to a third-person label for the same person within the same flow.

## 10. Cross-copy and Flow Consistency

Review related copy as a system, not only as isolated strings.

Check whether:

- The same concept has multiple names
- The same action uses inconsistent verbs
- Related screens describe the same status differently
- Titles, descriptions, buttons, toasts, and success messages contradict one another
- Earlier and later stages of the flow communicate conflicting expectations

Glossary-governed terminology issues belong to Layer 1.

Terminology or phrasing inconsistencies not governed by the Glossary may still be raised as a Layer 2 consistency issue.

## 11. Capitalization, Punctuation, and Formatting Consistency

Check consistency across related content for:

- Sentence case
- Title capitalization
- Punctuation
- Spacing
- Numbers
- Percentages
- Currency
- Dates
- Time
- Ranges
- Measurement units

If capitalization is explicitly governed by the Glossary or Style Guide, treat the issue as Layer 1 rather than Layer 2.

## 12. UX Pattern Appropriateness

Evaluate copy according to the role of the UI element.

### Titles

Titles should orient users and clearly identify the page, content, category, or task.

Prefer concise, descriptive wording and sentence case unless a reference specifies otherwise.

### Buttons and Links

Labels should clearly describe the resulting action.

Prefer specific action-oriented language such as:

- `Save changes`
- `Delete account`
- `View details`

Avoid generic labels such as `OK`, `Submit`, or `Click here` when a more specific action label would better set expectations.

Do not automatically reject a generic label when its meaning is already unambiguous in context.

### Error Messages

Help users understand:

1. What happened
2. Why, when useful and known
3. What they can do next

Avoid:

- Blaming the user
- Unexplained technical codes
- Vague dead ends
- Recovery instructions that are not actually available

### Success Messages

Clearly confirm what action was completed.

Keep routine confirmations brief and specific.

### Empty States

When useful, explain why the state is empty and provide a meaningful next action.

### Form Content

Use clear labels and instructions.

Do not rely on placeholder text as the only way to identify a field when persistent context is necessary.

### Notifications

Prioritize information according to urgency and required action.

Clearly distinguish between informational notifications and those requiring user action.

## 13. Voice and Tone Fit

Evaluate whether the tone is appropriate for the user's situation.

Consider:

- Purpose
- Context
- User emotional state
- Stakes of the action

Typical adaptations include:

- **Errors and failures:** clear, calm, and solution-focused
- **Complex or unfamiliar tasks:** patient and explanatory
- **Routine actions:** efficient and direct
- **High-stakes actions:** serious and transparent about consequences
- **Successful actions:** positive but proportional to the achievement

Do not introduce excessive humor, celebration, urgency, or emotional language when the context does not support it.

Where the Style Guide defines specific voice or tone requirements, Layer 1 takes precedence.

## 14. Accessibility and Comprehension

Review copy for language-related accessibility risks.

Check whether:

- Interactive labels make sense without relying entirely on surrounding visual context
- Link text describes its destination or action
- Error messages identify the relevant problem clearly
- Instructions are easy to scan
- Complex information is broken into manageable units
- Meaning does not depend only on color or visual treatment
- Idioms, metaphors, and culturally specific expressions create unnecessary comprehension barriers

Focus on issues that can be meaningfully evaluated from the available copy and context. Do not claim compliance with visual accessibility standards when layout or design information is unavailable.

## 15. Localization Readiness

Review copy for structures that may create unnecessary translation or internationalization risk.

Check for:

- Hard-coded English word order
- Sentence concatenation
- English-specific singular/plural constructions
- Placeholder-dependent grammar
- Ambiguous noun modifiers
- Idioms, wordplay, or cultural references that are difficult to translate
- Variables embedded in structures that may not work across languages

Prefer self-contained strings and structures that allow translators to reorder content naturally.

Do not rewrite clear, natural English solely because another language may use a different grammatical structure. Raise localization issues only when the source structure creates a concrete translation risk.

## Review Priority and Conflict Resolution

Use the following priority order:

1. **Legal Guidelines**
2. **Glossary and mandatory terminology requirements**
3. **Explicit Style Guide rules**
4. **Baseline Quality Check**

When Layer 1 and Layer 2 suggest different recommendations, Layer 1 takes precedence.

For example:

- Do not replace an approved Glossary term with a more conversational synonym.
- Do not shorten legally required information for conciseness.
- Do not change mandated capitalization to improve visual consistency.
- Do not apply a generic UX pattern when an explicit product-specific Style Guide rule requires a different pattern.

## Recommendation Standards

For every review finding:

- Identify a concrete issue or risk.
- Preserve the intended product meaning.
- Make the smallest effective change.
- Distinguish mandatory corrections from optional improvements.
- Avoid subjective rewrites that do not meaningfully improve quality.
- Do not manufacture feedback for copy that is already effective.

Use the following rationale labels:

- `Style Guide`
- `Glossary`
- `Game Legal Guidelines`
- `Revenue Legal Guidelines`
- `Baseline quality check`

When more than one principle supports the same recommendation, consolidate the finding rather than creating duplicate issues.

Whenever Layer 1 evidence is available, use it as the primary rationale.

If neither Layer 1 nor Layer 2 identifies a concrete issue, preserve the original copy.
