## **❖ What exactly is content design?**

Content design (also known as UX writing or UX content strategy) uses the words in a product interface to help people navigate through a product. These words can explain, educate, motivate, set expectations, show care and concern, provide feedback, and make the user comfortable using the product. If the words we use are confusing, the product will be confusing, because the user’s experience is shaped by both the design and the words that we choose.

A content designer is responsible for solving usability challenges in content first and foremost. They also consider tone, voice, grammar, spelling, terminology, and more. A content designer doesn’t solve every problem by adding text—in many cases, the content designer finds opportunities to remove unnecessary text. A content designer works hand in hand with the Product Team to build products, and also helps determine when and where information should be presented.

## ❖ Keep it short

App users often “scan” or “skim” content, so be concise and make every word count. Out of respect for our users and their time, prioritize brevity.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Can’t log in? Reset your password or contact us. | Having issues with logging in to your TikTok account? No worries! We are here to help. First, try to reset your password so you can log back in, and if that doesn’t work, then contact the TikTok team and see if they can help you with your account. |

## ❖ Share the most important information first

“Front-load” information to minimize confusion and information overload.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Reset your password in 3 steps | Here are three steps to resetting your TikTok password |

## ❖ Consider your user’s point of view

Talk about only what is relevant to your user. After writing something, ask yourself: “So what?” If the info isn’t obviously useful, don’t include it.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Your region won’t be shown publicly and may be used to customize your video experience. | Your region setting is non-public information, and we could use it to help customize your TikTok experience and it may be used in backend along with other factors to affect the content we are able to display. |

## ❖ Speak like a human

Use plain language that is easy to understand across all reading levels. Avoid jargon and use simple terms that are familiar to most people. If you must use a specialized term, briefly define it.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| A verified badge shows that an account is authentic. Verification isn’t guaranteed and it doesn’t mean that you’re endorsed by TikTok. | A verified badge is an exclusive symbol made visible to indicate the authentic presence of a notable public figure, e.g., a celebrity; however, the symbol does not qualify in any way as an endorsement from TikTok. |
| We’ll show videos related to other videos that you liked. | We’ll show you content in your feed that is optimized based on your preferences. |

## ❖ Don’t talk down to your users

Educate your users, but don’t patronize, accuse, or judge them.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| We don’t recognize this password. For your security, login isn’t available for the next 30 minutes. | You entered your password incorrectly multiple times and we believe that this activity is suspicious. We’re not going to let you log in for the next 30 minutes. |

## ❖ Be consistent

Consistency makes your product easier to understand. Each time you use a term or phrase appropriately and consistently, its meaning will be reinforced. If you switch to saying “music” instead of “sound,” then users will be uncertain if the new word refers to the same thing. If you use “Get Started” instead of “Next,” users may feel uncertain if tapping “Next” leads to the same result.

## ❖ Break up your content

For longer descriptions, try:

- Adding a **short, scannable heading**.
- **Use bulleted lists** instead of long paragraphs.
- **Adding line breaks** to break up your content. It can be difficult and time-consuming to parse long paragraphs of text.
- **Use progressive disclosure** to present only the necessary info for the user in the current step.

## ❖ Only use words when necessary

Consider whether you actually need words—in some cases, the UI might need a different design solution.

## ❖ Don’t be overly formal

Avoid using “please” in most UI copy. It adds unnecessary length and can feel impatient or insincere. Use it only when our product is at fault and the next steps may inconvenience the user.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Something went wrong. Please try again.
Couldn't upload image. Please try again. | Please enter a valid email address.
The passcode you entered is incorrect. Please try again. |

In addition, reserve “sorry” and other apologies only when the product causes a serious problem, such as inadvertently losing someone’s video as they’re in the process of editing or creating it. It’s overkill to apologize if the issue is simply a part of how the product works, such as when the user loses internet connection and can’t view a video.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Connect to the internet to view this video. | Sorry, you can’t view this video because you don’t have a network connection. We apologize and ask that you connect to the internet. |

## ❖ Don’t define actions as failures or successes.

Rather than tell someone they “failed” to complete an action, explain that an action couldn’t be completed and if possible, give them some context on how they can accomplish the task. Conversely, if the action is completed, saying it was accomplished “successfully” is redundant—either the task was completed or it wasn’t.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Couldn’t upload video. Connect to the internet and try again. | Your video upload failed. |

## ❖ Avoid overstatements and hyperbole

Try not to overstate claims: for example, calling TikTok the “best” app, telling users they’ll “never” see content that they don’t enjoy on the app, or that we “always” have the latest effects before any other platform. That’s not to say there are some superlative terms that can be appropriate, such as encouraging viewers to watch the “latest” videos on TikTok or check out our “top” sounds—but make sure it’s a claim that we can verify.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕\`** |
|-|-|
| Select “Not interested” and we’ll show you fewer videos like this in the future. | We’ll never show you a video that you don’t enjoy. |

## ❖ Be inclusive

We always aim to be inclusive: we use words to empower rather than to exclude. As a writer, think beyond an audience whose experience is exactly like yours, and actively challenge stereotypes and assumptions.

## ❖ Write for a global audience

TikTok is available to users around the world in many different languages. We use U.S. English as the source content for translation across all of those markets—which means it’s important to write user interface text that is easy to localize.

## ❖ How are voice and tone different?

No matter what the situation is or whom we’re speaking to, TikTok has the same voice throughout the product. Our voice is the personality of our product, and it doesn’t change. Our tone is the attitude we express through our words in any given situation, and it changes depending on the context.

---

## ❖ Voice

TikTok’s voice is **inclusive, approachable, conversational, and clear**. Our platform makes creators feel comfortable expressing themselves. We don’t take ourselves too seriously, and we keep it casual. We are secondary to the content on our platform, so our writing is simple, while letting our creators and their content shine.

---

## ❖ Tone

Our tone adjusts to fit the situation. Just as you'd change your tone when talking to a friend going through a difficult situation or when celebrating an important moment, we adjust our tone when guiding users through a new feature, when telling them that something isn't working, or when they've completed a task.

Backed by internal user research on tone of voice, our tone has two components: tone principles and a tone of voice framework. Our tone principles are high-level guidelines that define how we want to communicate with users. Meanwhile, our tone of voice framework provides instructions for how to apply our tone principles in specific scenarios.

#### **Tone principles**

1. **Foster positivity**

  Use positive language and highlight user benefits. Avoid threats or language around TikTok's needs.

1. **Empower users**

  Act as a supportive host rather than a strict instructor. Emphasize a sense of community where possible.

1. **Give context**

  Balance emotion with clarity. Consider what's relevant to users, and adjust your tone based on the situation.

1. **Capture visual attention**

  Use visual cues like exclamation marks and emoji appropriately. Avoid overwhelming or spammy visuals.

## ❖ Usage

#### **Abbreviations**

Avoid abbreviations, if possible—the full word is usually clearest. If necessary to shorten words for space, use these preferred abbreviations. No period is necessary, unless included in the example.

| **Do ✓** |
| Century | 21st |
| Dates | Jan 1 (not Jan 1st) |
| Days | Sun, Mon, Tues, Wed, Thurs, Fri, Sat, Sun Su, M, Tu, W, Th, F, Sa (if space is especially constrained) |
| Decades | 70s, 80s, 90s |
| File size | KB, MB, GB |
| Months | Jan, Feb, Mar, Apr, May, Jun, Jul, Aug, Sep, Oct, Nov, Dec |
| Numbers *(always capitalized)* | K = thousand ; M = million ; B = billion; |
| Country | U.S. (when referring to the United States) |
| Time *(always lowercase)* | w = weeks ; d = days ; h = hours ; m = minutes ; s = seconds; See timestamp outputs See related doc |
| Measurements | sq ft |
| Upload speeds | Bps, kbps, Mbps |

**Do not abbreviate**

- **Company or product names**: TikTok, ByteDance
- **Example**: Spell out the word ("for example"). Do not use "ex.", "e.g.", or "i.e."
- **Miscellaneous**: Do not use "misc."

#### **Acronyms**

Acronyms are often jargon and unfamiliar to most users, so use them only if there isn’t a common word to replace it. If you must use an acronym:

- Define the term in parentheses the first time you introduce it.

  - **Example**: API (Application Program Interface)
- Capitalize all letters of the acronym.

  - **Exception**: If referring to a file type, capitalize when used as a name (PNG), and use lowercase when used as an extension (.png).

#### **Active voice**

- *Active voice* highlights who or what is performing the action.
- *Passive voice* talks about the subject being acted upon.

| **Active voice** *(preferred)* | **Passive voice** |
|-|-|
| Eric uploaded a video. | The video was uploaded by Eric. |

In most cases, we use active voice because it keeps the meaning of the sentence clear and less complicated. We may use passive voice when it isn’t important who is performing an action (for example, if a video was flagged for violating TikTok guidelines).

#### **Articles**

In buttons, dialog titles with a call-to-action, or menu titles, you can remove articles. Articles include “a,” “an,” “the.”

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Upload video? | Upload a video? |

#### **Contractions**

Contractions replace at least one letter with an apostrophe (’) to make shortened words, such as: what’s, where’s, we’ll, we’re, you’ll, you’re, you’re, they’re, doesn’t, didn’t, isn’t, aren’t, or can’t.

- Contractions are almost always preferred because they make the content conversational.
- Don’t use colloquial contractions, such as: ain’t, y’all
- Don’t use these less common contractions: would’ve, could’ve, it’ll
- Be careful when using contracting “is,” “does,” “has,” or “was,” as they may resemble the possessive form of nouns and be less readable.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| maxattacks is currently LIVE | maxattacks’s currently LIVE |

- Be careful when using contractions in more sensitive or serious messages, such as an error message or account suspension notice. In such cases, it may be better to avoid contractions to prioritize clarity and to be respectful of the situation.

#### **Prefixes**

A prefix is added in front of a word to create a new word. By adding a prefix, you enhance or change the meaning of the word.

Avoid using prefixes whenever possible in headings, body copy, labels etc, and spell out the words instead. Spelling out the words in full will also minimise localization difficulties.

- For example, “instantly generated” instead of “insta-generated” or “instagenerated”.

Make exceptions to this rule whenever necessary to adhere to space constraints, or if the word is part of the feature’s name, or commonly used alongside a feature.

- For example, “auto-generated captions” in place of “automatically generated captions”, and “multi-guest” instead of "multiple guests".

#### **Pronouns**

- In most situations, use the second person (“you”) to address the TikTok user.

  - **Example**: When’s your birthday?
- While we typically should avoid using first person ("my" or "I") in UX text because switching points of view can confuse the user, using I/me/my may be acceptable in some cases when the experience is referring to the user's point of view or is signifying ownership or relation to the user.

  - Some examples of instances to use first person include secondary CTAs (such as "remind me later"), privacy, settings, verification, reporting or appeals, social or sharing, FAQs, and surveys.
  - **Examples of instances to use first person:**

    - “Who will use this account?” CTA 1: “Me” CTA 2: “Someone else”
    - “View my Q&A” (vs View Q&A)
    - "I have permission from the rightholder to use this content on TikTok" (appeal)
    - "I confirm I'm the parent or guardian of @username" (family pairing confirmation)
- Try avoiding unnecessary pronouns (unless it interferes with the tone of the messaging).

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Edit profile | Edit my profile |

## ❖ Formatting

Typographical changes for emphasis should be used sparingly since they can hinder readability and accessibility. The following is guidance for how to use bolded, italicized, and underlined text.

#### **Bolding**

Use bolded text sparingly, as it won’t be picked up by screen readers. Bolded text should only be used to:

- Reinforce information hierarchy (such as section titles)
- Make content more scannable when needed
- Differentiate the names of UI elements from surrounding text. **Example**: “Remove from **Saved”**

  - If the name of a UI element is included in a phrase that’s already bold and hyperlinked, discuss a preferred formatting method with your engineer, as there currently isn’t a consensus among development teams.

#### **Capitalization**

📌 Sentence case | Title case | Proper nouns

`Sentence case`**` `**

We use sentence case for most of our UI text because it’s easier to read, helps proper nouns stand out, and makes our content look friendlier and more casual.

- Use sentence case: capitalize only the first word and all proper nouns in a string, regardless of whether it is a phrase or full sentence.

  - **Note:** Names of effects and certain features are in title case if they are proper nouns.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Try the Duet feature to collaborate with another creator. | Try the Duet Feature to Collaborate with Another Creator. |
| Link copied | Link Copied |

- If a string starts with a number, don’t capitalize the first word.

  - **Example**: 5 new messages
- Do not use all caps (for example, “TRY THIS NOW”). This is generally viewed as if someone was yelling.

  - **Exception**: All caps might be a visual design standard in some parts of our UI, such as for section labels or content tags. Consult with your friendly neighborhood designer or product manager to verify the visual styling for a specific type of content.

`Title case`

Title case is used only for category names or items within a list. You may see these category names in the interest selector, within a list of categories in a taxonomy, or suggested topics.

**Example:**

!图片展示了TikTok应用中“Choose your interests”页面，上方标题为“Choose your interests Get better video recommendations”，下方有五个兴趣分类选项，分别为带有小狗头像的“Pets”、带有口红头像的“Beauty & Fashion”、带有笑脸头像的“Funny”、带有电影胶片头像的“Vlogs”以及带有面具头像的“Drama & Acting”。该图片与文档中“Title case”部分内容相关，用于说明Title case仅用于类别名称或列表中的项目，如在兴趣选择器中看到的类别名称等。

`Proper nouns`

- Capitalize proper nouns when they are used anywhere in the user interface. This helps a proper noun stand out and distinguishes it from other meanings it may have as a common noun.
- In legal documentation, key terms that are normally common nouns may look like proper nouns because they are capitalized to help them stand out, but make sure that common nouns still follow our capitalization rules in the user interface.
- Review our terms glossary to see proper capitalization of product and feature names.

#### **Italics**

Avoid using italicized text unless required. It can be difficult to read, especially for users with dyslexia and visual impairments.

#### **Lists**

Lists can make your content more scannable.

- For your list to be localized, start with an introductory phrase that is a full sentence with a colon. Try to avoid repeating phrases—if something applies to the entire list, try adding it to the introductory phrase.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
| Your viewers can find your videos from the following sources:For You; Following; Discover; | Your viewers can find your videos from For You feed; Following feed; Discover; |

- Use parallel construction for all items in your list. For example, if you’re describing several actions that each start with a verb, make sure this applies to all items in the list.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
| You can make these changes from your profile:Edit your bio; Add a nickname; Change your username; | You can make these changes from your profile:Edit your bio; Nicknames: Edit or add; You can change your username; |

- Use bulleted lists for concepts of equal weight. Use numbers for a series of steps.

#### **Underlining**

Avoid using underlined text. It can have a negative impact on readability and design.

- **Exception**: Hyperlinked text can be underlined.

## ❖ Numbers

#### **Big and small numbers**

- Use numerals, instead of spelling out the numbers.

  - **Example**: “2” instead of “two”
- Leave a space between a number and a related unit of measurement.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| 512 KB | 512KB |

- Use a comma in numbers of 4 digits or more.

  - **Example**: “1,500” and “100,000”
  - **Exceptions:**

    - Due to space constraints, don’t use a comma in the like, comment, favorite, and share counts on the in-app feeds.

  | **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
  |-|-|
  | Michael and 2,000 others liked your video | Michael and two thousand others liked your video |
- Default to the long format for numbers of 4 to 7 digits. The short format can cause issues for localization and readability. We also currently do not localize the short format because the L10N and engineering teams do not yet have a number localization solution built out.

  - **Example:** “15,500” (long format) vs. “15.5K” (short format)
  - **Exceptions:**

    - Only use the short format if it’s the only way to stay within a character limit.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Profile views
      5,100,000 |       Profile views5.1M |

- Default to the short format for extremely big numbers of 8 or more digits. In this context, the long format can cause readability issues because most people can't interpret that many digits in numeric form.

#### **Currency**

- In a UI that depicts financial transactions, include the full numerical form.

  - **Example**: \$1.00 or \$28.95
- In a conversational description, the format \$1 can be used.

  - **Example**: \$1, \$30
- If necessary, include the currency symbol and the currency type.

  - **Example**: \$1.00 USD
- Use an en dash to specify a price range.

  - **Example**: \$1 – \$20

#### **Dates**

- Write out the entire date, if you have the space.

  - **Example**: April 12, 2020
- Avoid ordinal numbers, such as “first”, “1st”, “23rd”, or “14th”.
- When describing a date range that includes the year, include the year in both dates and an en dash.

  - **Example**: Jan 1, 2018 – Feb 1, 2018
- Use slashes when showing all-numeric dates (as opposed to the hyphen “-” or period “.”).

  - **Example**: 12/30/2020
- If you abbreviate a date, don’t include a “0” before a single-digit month.

  - **Example**: 5/30/2019
- Don’t abbreviate months if they’re not preceding a date.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| Watch trending videos from September | Watch trending videos from Sept |

- Use numerals for years.

  - **Example**: 1950, 2050

#### **Measurements**

`Dimension & Resolution`

- Use the multiplication sign (×) when writing about interface sizes. Avoid the use of the letter “x” or the word “by.”

| **Do ✓**  | **Don’t ×** |
|-|-|
| Minimum: 480 × 360 pixels | Minimum: 480 x 360 pixels |

- Add a space before and after the multiplication sign. Add a space before “pixels.”

  - **Example**:  Upload images that are 256 × 256 pixels.

#### **Percentages, decimals, and fractions**

- Write out decimals to the hundredths decimal place.
- Write out fractions using “of” or “out of” instead of “/”.

  - **Example**: 1 of 5

#### **Telephone numbers**

Write out phone numbers with the area code in parentheses and the remaining number separated by an en dash.

- **Example**: (000) 000-0000

## **❖ Inclusion**

#### **Age**

- Don’t use age descriptors like “young,” “old,” or “elderly.”
- Don’t use ageist stereotypes (such as, “millenials always \_\_\_\_,” “old people are \_\_\_\_\_”).

| **Category** | **Don’t ✕** |
| Age descriptors | Young; Old; Elderly; |
| Ageist stereotypes | Millenials always...; Old people are...; |

#### **Disability**

- Don’t refer to a subset or subsets of people as “normal.”
- Avoid “ableist” language. Don’t use idioms or metaphors that may stigmatize, such as “lame,” “crippling,” “blind,” “paralyzed,” “deaf,” “crazy,” etc.
- Avoid terms that reflect or project feelings and judgements about a person’s disability, such as: “victim of” or “suffering from.” Instead, use neutral terms such as: “experiencing” or “living with.”
- Do not use disabilities as nouns to refer to people. For example, use “person with a disability” instead of “disabled person.”
- Make content more accessible to people with vision or hearing disabilities wherever possible.

#### **Gender and sexuality**

- Avoid gendered terms. Instead, use gender-neutral terms.
- Always use someone’s preferred pronoun.

  - In instances where you are unaware of the preferred pronoun, try to avoid using a pronoun by using their name. It is also OK to use “they” as a singular, non-gendered pronoun.
- Don’t refer to “same-sex” marriage or “gay marriage” (unless the distinction is necessary in what you’re writing). Just use “marriage.”
- Instead of “men and women,” say “all people.”
- Don’t refer to groups of people as “guys”. Don’t refer to women as “girls.”
- Give people the ability to self-identify rather than limiting them to a set list of options.

| **Category** | **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
| Prounouns | {username} accepted your request; If you block their account, they won’t be able to send you messages.; | He accepted your request; If you block her account, she won’t be able to send you messages.; |
| Gendered terms | Postal worker ; Firefighter; Congressperson; | Postman; Fireman; Congressman; |
| Marriage | Marriage; | Same-sex marriage; Gay marriage; |
| Groups of people | All people; Everyone; | Men and women; Guys ; Girls; |

#### **Heritage and nationality**

- First: Check whether ethnicity, race, heritage, or nationality should even be mentioned in the context that you’re writing for.
- If it is applicable to mention ethnicity, race, heritage, or nationality:

  - Don’t use hyphens when referring to someone with dual heritage or nationality. For example, use “African American” instead of “African-American.”
  - Use gender-neutral terms, such as “Latinx” instead of “Latino/Latina.”
  - If applicable, consider the specificity of the term required in the context that you’re writing for. For example, “people of Thai descent” vs. “Asians.”

#### **Intuitive bias**

Intuitive bias is a form of bias that affects our judgements and decision making. It can influence our content decision process, which in turn will affect the user experience or perception of a choice outcome. Therefore, as much as possible, we should avoid channeling the user to perceive interactions in a certain way, or assume a sufficient level of ability to do something. Avoid making surface-level assumptions about our users and provide clear, objective guidance when interacting with our product.

- **Check if your content contains ambiguity**: For example, is “popular talent” an applicable term for your target audience? Is an estimated completion time necessary?
- **Check for cognitive assumptions**: When users need to perform an action, such as filling out a form, check if there is additional information that could help them complete the task.
- **Be aware of multiple perspectives**: Verb- or adjective-heavy strings might channel users to perceive interactions in a certain way. Only include descriptors if they are helpful to users.
- **Test your content**: When in doubt, share your content with other content designers to validate whether the content is best perceived as intended, to look out for possible intuitive bias, and to provide constructive feedback.

## **❖ Localization**

- Again, keep it **short**. Some languages may take up twice the amount of space that the English text uses.
- Write in active voice when possible (and depending on the context).
- Use **subject-verb-object** sentence structure—it’s not universal to every language, but it is widely understood.
- Avoid **“-ing”** words. Words ending in “-ing” can be nouns, adjectives, or verbs, so it can be difficult to translate them.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕**     |
|-|-|
| Follow an account to see more of their videos. | Following accounts means you’ll see more of their videos. |

- Avoid using “has” / “have” and a past participle. It can be confusing to understand if you’re referring to an action or to ownership.

| **Do ✓** | **Don’t ✕** |
|-|-|
| This account contains blocked videos. | This account has blocked videos. |

- **Avoid idioms and slang**—they often don’t translate.
- **Avoid words with double meanings**—for example, “right” can mean correct, a direction (the opposite of “left,” or politically conservative).
- **Avoid referencing anything that is of local or regional importance** that may not be known to people outside of the U.S.
- **Don’t reference the “dollar”**—instead, use the appropriate currency symbol and make sure this is localized for each market.
- **Defer to the American spelling** of a word for U.S. English source content.

# Naming

We support product teams in creating product and feature names through “namestorms”.

 ↗ See ,  and namestorm template

## Types of names

In the TikTok product, names will generally fall into one of these categories:

- **Generic**The common term for a feature or product. Not capitalized and usually a common noun. Most names should fall within this category. For example: direct messages, notifications, email, captions, sounds, templates
- **Descriptive**A name that describes a product’s functionality or characteristics. For example: Digital Wellbeing, Data Saver, Creator Portal, Family Pairing, TikTok Creator Fund
- **Evocative/suggestive**A name that invokes an image and that can be more abstract. For example: Stitch, Duet, Scenes, TikTok Shoutouts, TikTok Creator Fund
- **Constructed/fanciful/arbitrary**In other words, a made-up name or a word that has a meaning that has nothing to do with the product, but has come to represent it. Generally, this would be reserved for a proprietary feature or product. Can be used to help differentiate your product from competitors to make it more distinct and “ownable”. Often a proper noun, but doesn’t have to be. For example: TikTok, Apple, Kodak

## Naming guidelines

- **Step 1: Ensure that your PM is following the**

  - If this is a Tier 1 product or P0 feature, ensure your PM has engaged Brand and PMM to oversee this end-to-end.
- **Step 2: Decide if a name is necessary**

Most of the time, you don't need to name a feature. It may be easier for users to understand the feature when you describe the feature or action itself, or use a generic or industry-standard term. If a name is necessary:

- PM should start a Lark group chat involving all XFN stakeholders right from the beginning.
- Ensure that PM has agreed on the timeline, as trademark checks take time.

- **Step 3: Start listing name options using** **this template**

Things to note
-  "TikTok" must not be split up into "Tik" or "Tok" for new feature names as this dilutes the brand. "Edutok" and "Booktok" are legacy names and are exceptions (this is mandated by the IP team and global head of legal )
- Avoid using internal-only names or acronyms.
- Avoid names that are used in other industries. If it suits your product feature well and you'd like to push for it, state clearly in the notes section that the proposed name exists and in what industry, so that stakeholders are aware.
- Avoid names that may have negative or strange connotations that could contribute to legal or PR issues.
- Ensure the name does not clash with other terminology across TikTok.
- Check with PM and your product designer for character count.
- Check with localization if a name translates well.

- **Step 4: Get feedback and votes at Content Forum**

Share your naming document and get feedback and votes from the content design team either during the session or in the Lark group. You can use this as additional rationale when presenting final recommendations to stakeholders.

- **Step 5: Share the shortlist of names**

  Share the document of shortlisted names with rationales, pros and cons in the Lark group chat with all stakeholders.

- **Step 6: Conduct user tests (Optional)**

  Pick the top 3 names to conduct a quick qualitative usertesting.com and quantitative live AB experiment to see which name resonates best with your audience.

#### Effects, filters, stickers naming

- **Use title case** for effect names.
- **Keep names descriptive and straightforward**, and try to keep names under 3-4 words and 20 characters.
- **Name similar things similarly**. For example, if you have several “split cam” effects, use similar names so that people can rely on their prior knowledge of another split cam effect they may have used.

## Guidelines for writing in-app FAQs for a feature

### What are in-app FAQs for a feature?

In-app FAQs for a feature are a list of questions that users might ask about a particular feature. They usually include:

- a brief introduction of a feature
- step-by-step instructions to use the feature
- questions that are most likely to be raised by users
- encouraged or prohibited user behavior

## Legal

Legal content may strike a more serious tone than the rest of our user interface text. But that being said, try to use plain language and pare down the legal jargon to just what’s necessary.

### Legal review

You may need legal review for some features or content to help identify any risks or to make sure it complies with current legislation. You can contact our U.S. legal point of contact, , to request a review from someone on the Legal team.

To view best practices on how to work with the legal team, view this doc:

**Content that may require legal review**

*(Including, but not limited to, this list)*

- Terms of Service (preferred term) / Terms of Use / Terms and Conditions
- Legal disclaimers
- Policies, including, but not limited to:

  - Acceptable Use Policy
  - Privacy Policy
  - API Use Policy
  - Copyright Policy
- Content that requires a user to disclose potentially sensitive information (such as birthday, age, driver’s license number)
